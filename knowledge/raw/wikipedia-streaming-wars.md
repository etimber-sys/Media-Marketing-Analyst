This article is about streaming media in general. For streaming platform, see [Over-the-top media service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-top_media_service "Over-the-top media service"). For the music listening format specifically, see [Music streaming service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_streaming_service "Music streaming service").

**Streaming media** is the method by which [multimedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimedia "Multimedia") is delivered through a [network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network") for playback using a [digital media player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media_player "Digital media player") or [media player software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_player_software "Media player software"). Media is transferred in a _stream_ of [packets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_packet "Network packet") from a [server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_(computing) "Server (computing)") to a [client](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client-server_model "Client-server model") and is rendered in real-time or [near real-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_real-time "Near real-time");[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-1) this contrasts with file [downloading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downloading "Downloading"), a process in which the end-user obtains an entire media file before consuming the content. Streaming is more commonly used for [video on demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand "Video on demand"), [streaming television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_television "Streaming television"), and [music streaming services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_streaming_service "Music streaming service") over the Internet.

While streaming is most commonly associated with multimedia from a remote server over the Internet, it also includes [offline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offline "Offline") multimedia between devices on a [local area network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_area_network "Local area network"). For example, using [DLNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DLNA "DLNA")[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-2) and a [home server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_server "Home server"), or in a [personal area network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_area_network "Personal area network") between two devices using [Bluetooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth "Bluetooth") (which uses [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_waves "Radio waves") rather than [IP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Protocol "Internet Protocol")).[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-3) Online streaming was initially popularized by [RealNetworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RealNetworks "RealNetworks") and [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft") in the 1990s[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-4) and has since grown to become the globally most popular method for consuming music and videos,[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-5) with numerous competing subscription services being offered since the 2010s.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-StreamingWars-6) Audio streaming to [wireless speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_speaker "Wireless speaker"), often using Bluetooth, is another use that has become prevalent during that decade.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-7) [Live streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_streaming "Live streaming") is the real-time delivery of content during production, much as [live television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_television "Live television") broadcasts content via television channels.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-8)

Distinguishing delivery methods from the media applies specifically to, as most of the traditional media delivery systems are either inherently _streaming_ (e.g., radio, television) or inherently _non-streaming_ (e.g., books, [videotapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape "Videotape"), [audio CDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_CD "Audio CD")). The term "streaming media" can apply to media other than video and audio, such as live [closed captioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_captioning "Closed captioning") and [real-time text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_text "Real-time text"), which are all considered "streaming text".

## Etymology

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology")\]

The term "streaming" was first used for [tape drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_drive "Tape drive") manufactured by Data Electronics Inc. that were meant to slowly ramp up and run for the entire track; slower ramp times lowered drive costs. "Streaming" was applied in the early 1990s as a better description for [video on demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand "Video on demand") and later live video on [IP networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_network "IP network"). It was first done by [Starlight Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlight_Networks "Starlight Networks") for video streaming and [Real Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Networks "Real Networks") for audio streaming. Such video had previously been referred to by the misnomer "store and forward video."[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-:2-9)

## Precursors

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Precursors")\]

Beginning in 1881, [Théâtrophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9%C3%A2trophone "Théâtrophone") enabled subscribers to listen to opera and theatre performances over telephone lines. This operated until 1932. The [Telharmonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telharmonium "Telharmonium") was patented in 1897. The concept of media streaming eventually came to America.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-10) The [Tel-musici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tel-musici "Tel-musici") service operated in Delaware from 1909 to 1914.

In the early 1920s, [George Owen Squier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Owen_Squier "George Owen Squier") was granted patents for a system for the transmission and distribution of signals over electrical lines,[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-11) which was the technical basis for what later became _[Muzak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzak "Muzak")_, a technology for streaming continuous music to commercial customers without the use of radio.

The Telephone Music Service, a live jukebox service, began in 1929 and continued until 1997.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-12)[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-pittsburghmagazine.com-13) The clientele eventually included 120 bars and restaurants in the Pittsburgh area. A tavern customer would deposit money in the jukebox, use a telephone on top of the jukebox, and ask the operator to play a song. The operator would find the record in the studio library of more than 100,000 records, put it on a turntable, and the music would be piped over the telephone line to play in the tavern. The music media began as 78s, 33s and 45s, played on the six turntables they monitored. CDs and tapes were incorporated in later years.

The business had a succession of owners, notably Bill Purse, his daughter Helen Reutzel, and finally Dotti White. The revenue stream for each quarter was split between 60% for the music service and 40% for the tavern owner.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-14) This business model eventually became unsustainable due to city permits and the cost of setting up these telephone lines.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-pittsburghmagazine.com-13)

## History

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: History")\]

### Early development

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Early development")\]

See also: [Timeline of online video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_online_video "Timeline of online video")

Attempts to display media on [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer "Personal computer") date back to the earliest days of computing in the mid-20th century. However, little progress was made for several decades, primarily due to the high cost and limited capabilities of computer hardware. From the late 1980s through the 1990s, consumer-grade personal computers became powerful enough to display various media. The primary technical issues related to streaming were having enough [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU "CPU") and [bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_(computing) "Bus (computing)") [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_(computing) "Bandwidth (computing)") to support the required data rates and achieving the [real-time computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_computing "Real-time computing") performance required to prevent [buffer underruns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_underrun "Buffer underrun") and enable smooth streaming of the content. However, computer networks were still limited in the mid-1990s, and audio and video media were usually delivered over non-streaming channels, such as playback from a local [hard disk drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive "Hard disk drive") or [CD-ROMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD-ROM "CD-ROM") on the end user's computer.

Terminology in the 1970s was at best confusing for applications such as telemetered aircraft or missile test data. By then PCM \[Pulse Code Modulation\] was the dominant transmission type. This PCM transmission was bit-serial and not packetized so the 'streaming' terminology was often a confusion factor. In 1969 Grumman acquired one of the first telemetry ground stations \[Automated Telemetry Station, 'ATS'\] which had the capability for reconstructing serial telemetered data which had been recorded on digital computer peripheral tapes. Computer peripheral tapes were inherently recorded in blocks. Reconstruction was required for continuous display purposes without time-base distortion. The Navy implemented similar capability in DoD for the first time in 1973. These implementations are the only known examples of true 'streaming' in the sense of reconstructing distortion-free serial data from packetized or blocked recordings.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-15) 'Real-time' terminology has also been confusing in streaming context. The most accepted definition of 'real-time' requires that all associated processing or formatting of the data must take place prior to availability of the next sample of each measurement. In the 1970s the most powerful mainframe computers were not fast enough for this task at significant overall data rates in the range of 50,000 samples per second. For that reason both the Grumman ATS and the Navy Real-time Telemetry Processing System \[RTPS\] employed unique special purpose digital computers dedicated to real-time processing of raw data samples.

In 1990, the first commercial [Ethernet switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet_switch "Ethernet switch") was introduced by [Kalpana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalpana,_Inc. "Kalpana, Inc."), which enabled the more powerful computer networks that led to the first streaming video solutions used by schools and corporations.

Practical streaming media was only made possible with advances in [data compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression") due to the impractically high bandwidth requirements of uncompressed media. Raw [digital audio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_audio "Digital audio") encoded with [pulse-code modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse-code_modulation "Pulse-code modulation") (PCM) requires a bandwidth of 1.4 [Mbit/s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbit/s "Mbit/s") for uncompressed [CD audio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD_audio "CD audio"), while raw [digital video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_video "Digital video") requires a bandwidth of 168Mbit/s for [SD video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SD_video "SD video") and over 1000Mbit/s for [FHD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FHD "FHD") video.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-16)

### Late 1990s to early 2000s

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Late 1990s to early 2000s")\]

See also: [Original net animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_net_animation "Original net animation")

During the late 1990s and early 2000s, users had increased access to computer networks, especially the Internet. During the early 2000s, users had access to increased network [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_(signal_processing) "Bandwidth (signal processing)"), especially in the [last mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_mile_(telecommunications) "Last mile (telecommunications)"). These technological improvements facilitated the streaming of audio and video content to computer users in their homes and workplaces. There was also an increasing use of standard protocols and formats, such as [TCP/IP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCP/IP "TCP/IP"), [HTTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP "HTTP"), and [HTML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML "HTML"), as the Internet became increasingly commercialized, which led to an infusion of investment into the sector.

The band [Severe Tire Damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severe_Tire_Damage_(band) "Severe Tire Damage (band)") was the first group to perform live on the Internet. On 24 June 1993, the band was playing a gig at [Xerox PARC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_PARC "Xerox PARC"), while elsewhere in the building, scientists were discussing new technology (the [Mbone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbone "Mbone")) for broadcasting on the Internet using [multicasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multicast "Multicast"). As proof of PARC's technology, the band's performance was broadcast and could be seen live in Australia and elsewhere. In a March 2017 interview, band member Russ Haines stated that the band had used approximately "half of the total bandwidth of the internet" to stream the performance, which was a 152 × 76 pixel video, updated eight to twelve times per second, with audio quality that was, "at best, a bad telephone connection."[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-17) In October 1994, a school music festival was webcast from the Michael Fowler Centre in Wellington, New Zealand. The technician who arranged the webcast, local council employee Richard Naylor, later commented: "We had 16 viewers in 12 countries."[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-18)

[RealNetworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RealNetworks "RealNetworks") pioneered the broadcast of a baseball game between the [New York Yankees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Yankees "New York Yankees") and the [Seattle Mariners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle_Mariners "Seattle Mariners") over the Internet in 1995.[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-19) The first symphonic concert on the Internet—a collaboration between the [Seattle Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle_Symphony "Seattle Symphony") and guest musicians [Slash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_(musician) "Slash (musician)"), [Matt Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Cameron "Matt Cameron"), and [Barrett Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrett_Martin "Barrett Martin")—took place at the [Paramount Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramount_Theatre_(Seattle,_Washington) "Paramount Theatre (Seattle, Washington)") in [Seattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle "Seattle"), Washington, on 10 November 1995.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-20)

In 1996, [Marc Scarpa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Scarpa "Marc Scarpa") produced the first large-scale, online, live broadcast, the [Adam Yauch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Yauch "Adam Yauch")–led [Tibetan Freedom Concert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Freedom_Concert "Tibetan Freedom Concert"), an event that would define the format of social change broadcasts. Scarpa continued to pioneer in the streaming media world with projects such as [Woodstock '99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock_%2799 "Woodstock '99"), Townhall with [President Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Bill_Clinton "Presidency of Bill Clinton"), and more recently Covered CA's campaign "Tell a Friend Get Covered", which was livestreamed on YouTube.

### Business developments

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Business developments")\]

[Xing Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xing_Technology "Xing Technology") was founded in 1989 and developed a JPEG streaming product called "StreamWorks". Another streaming product appeared in late 1992 and was named StarWorks.[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-StarWorks-21) StarWorks enabled on-demand MPEG-1 full-motion videos to be randomly accessed on corporate [Ethernet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet "Ethernet") networks. Starworks was from [Starlight Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlight_Networks "Starlight Networks"), which also pioneered live video streaming on Ethernet and via [Internet Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Protocol "Internet Protocol") over satellites with [Hughes Network Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hughes_Network_Systems "Hughes Network Systems").[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-Starlight_Networks_and_Hughes_Network_Systems-22) Other early companies that created streaming media technology include Progressive Networks and Protocomm prior to widespread World Wide Web usage. After the [Netscape IPO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netscape_IPO "Netscape IPO") in 1995 (and the release of [Windows 95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_95 "Windows 95") with built-in [TCP/IP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCP/IP "TCP/IP") support), usage of the Internet expanded, and [many companies "went public"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble "Dot-com bubble"), including Progressive Networks (which was renamed " [RealNetworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RealNetworks "RealNetworks")", and listed on [Nasdaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasdaq "Nasdaq") as "RNWK"). As the web became even more popular in the late 90s, streaming video on the internet blossomed from startups such as [Vivo Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivo_Software "Vivo Software") (later acquired by RealNetworks), VDOnet (acquired by RealNetworks), Precept (acquired by [Cisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisco "Cisco")), and Xing (acquired by RealNetworks).[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-23)

[Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft") developed a media player known as [ActiveMovie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ActiveMovie "ActiveMovie") in 1995 that supported streaming media and included a proprietary streaming format, which was the precursor to the streaming feature later in [Windows Media Player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Media_Player "Windows Media Player") 6.4 in 1999. In June 1999, [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc. "Apple Inc.") also introduced a streaming media format in its [QuickTime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QuickTime "QuickTime") 4 application. It was later also widely adopted on websites, along with RealPlayer and Windows Media streaming formats. The competing formats on websites required each user to download the respective applications for streaming, which resulted in many users having to have all three applications on their computer for general compatibility.

In 2000, Industryview.com launched its "world's largest streaming video archive" website to help businesses promote themselves.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-24) Webcasting became an emerging tool for business marketing and advertising that combined the immersive nature of television with the interactivity of the Web. The ability to collect data and feedback from potential customers caused this technology to gain momentum quickly.[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-25)

Around 2002, the interest in a single, unified, streaming format and the widespread adoption of [Adobe Flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Flash "Adobe Flash") prompted the development of a video streaming format through Flash, which was the format used in Flash-based players on [video hosting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_hosting "Video hosting") sites. The first popular video streaming site, YouTube, was founded by [Steve Chen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Chen "Steve Chen"), [Chad Hurley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chad_Hurley "Chad Hurley"), and [Jawed Karim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jawed_Karim "Jawed Karim") in 2005. It initially used a Flash-based player, which played [MPEG-4 AVC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPEG-4_AVC "MPEG-4 AVC") video and [AAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Audio_Coding "Advanced Audio Coding") audio, but now defaults to [HTML video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML_video "HTML video").[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-26) Increasing consumer demand for live streaming prompted YouTube to implement a new live streaming service for users.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-27) The company currently also offers a (secure) link that returns the available connection speed of the user.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-28)

The [Recording Industry Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_Industry_Association_of_America "Recording Industry Association of America") (RIAA) revealed through its 2015 earnings report that streaming services were responsible for 34.3 percent of the year's total [music industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_industry "Music industry")'s revenue, growing 29 percent from the previous year and becoming the largest source of income, pulling in around $2.4 billion.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-29)[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-30) US streaming revenue grew 57 percent to $1.6 billion in the first half of 2016 and accounted for almost half of industry sales.[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-31)

### Streaming wars

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Streaming wars")\]

See also: [List of streaming media services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_streaming_media_services "List of streaming media services")

"Streaming wars" redirects here. For the South Park film, see [South Park: The Streaming Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Park:_The_Streaming_Wars "South Park: The Streaming Wars").

For competition between music streaming platforms, see [§ Music streaming platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#Music_streaming_platforms).

The term _streaming wars_ was coined to describe the new era (starting in the late 2010s) of competition between video streaming services such as [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix "Netflix"), [Amazon Prime Video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Prime_Video "Amazon Prime Video"), [Hulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulu "Hulu"), [HBO Max](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO_Max "HBO Max"), [Disney+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney%2B "Disney+"), [Paramount+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramount%2B "Paramount+"), [Apple TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_TV%2B "Apple TV+"), [Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_(streaming_service) "Peacock (streaming service)"), and many more.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-StreamingWars-6)[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-32)

The competition between increasingly popular online platforms, such as Netflix and Amazon, and legacy broadcasters and studios moving online, like Disney and NBC, has driven each service to find ways to differentiate from one another. A key differentiator has been offering exclusive content, often self-produced and created for a specific [market segment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_segment "Market segment").

When Netflix first launched in 2007, it became one of the more dominant streaming platforms even though it initially offered no original content. It would be nearly six years before Netflix began offering its own shows, such as [House of Cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Cards_(American_TV_series) "House of Cards (American TV series)"), [Orange Is the New Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_Is_the_New_Black "Orange Is the New Black"), and [Hemlock Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemlock_Grove_(TV_series) "Hemlock Grove (TV series)"). The legacy services also began producing original digital-only content, but they also began restricting their back catalog of shows and movies to their platforms, one of the most notable examples being Disney+. Disney took advantage of owning popular movies and shows like Frozen, Snow White, and the Star Wars and [Marvel franchises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel_Cinematic_Universe "Marvel Cinematic Universe"), which could draw in more subscribers and make it a more serious competitor to Netflix and Amazon.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-33) Research suggests that this approach to streaming competition can be disadvantageous for consumers by increasing spending across platforms, and for the industry as a whole by dilution of subscriber base.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] Once specific content is made available on a streaming service, piracy searches for the same content decrease; competition or legal availability across multiple platforms appears to deter online piracy. Exclusive content produced for subscription services such as Netflix tends to have a higher production budget than content produced exclusively for [pay-per-view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pay-per-view "Pay-per-view") services, such as Amazon Prime Video.[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-34)

This competition increased during the first two years of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic") as more people stayed home and watched TV. "The COVID-19 pandemic has led to a seismic shift in the film & TV industry in terms of how films are made, distributed, and screened. Many industries have been hit by the economic effects of the pandemic" (Totaro Donato).[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-:2-9) In August 2022, a CNN headline declared that "The streaming wars are over" as pandemic-era restrictions had largely ended and audience growth had stalled. This led services to focus on profit over market share by cutting production budgets, cracking down on password sharing, and introducing ad-supported tiers.[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-35) A December 2022 article in _[The Verge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Verge "The Verge")_ echoed this, declaring an end to the "golden age of the streaming wars".[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-36)

In September 2023, several streaming services formed a [trade association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_association "Trade association") named the Streaming Innovation Alliance (SIA), spearheaded by [Charles Rivkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Rivkin "Charles Rivkin") of the [Motion Picture Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_Picture_Association "Motion Picture Association") (MPA). Former [U.S. representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._representative "U.S. representative") [Fred Upton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Upton "Fred Upton") and former [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission "Federal Communications Commission") (FCC) acting chair [Mignon Clyburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mignon_Clyburn "Mignon Clyburn") serve as senior advisors. ## Use by the general public

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Use by the general public")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/%E7%BE%BD%E6%9D%91%E5%B8%82%E5%8B%95%E7%89%A9%E5%85%AC%E5%9C%92_%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E7%94%9F_2017_%2832782507624%29.jpg/250px-%E7%BE%BD%E6%9D%91%E5%B8%82%E5%8B%95%E7%89%A9%E5%85%AC%E5%9C%92_%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E7%94%9F_2017_%2832782507624%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:%E7%BE%BD%E6%9D%91%E5%B8%82%E5%8B%95%E7%89%A9%E5%85%AC%E5%9C%92_%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E7%94%9F_2017_(32782507624).jpg) A camera [live streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_streaming "Live streaming") at a zoo by [Niconico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niconico "Niconico")

Advances in [computer networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"), combined with powerful home computers and operating systems, have made streaming media affordable and easy for the public. Stand-alone [Internet radio devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_radio_device "Internet radio device") emerged to offer listeners a non-technical option for listening to audio streams. These audio-streaming services became increasingly popular; music streaming reached 4 trillion streams globally in 2023—a significant increase from 2022—jumping 34% over the year.[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-39)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Bluetooth_Music_Streaming_-_2014_Scion_tC_%2815664918268%29.jpg/250px-Bluetooth_Music_Streaming_-_2014_Scion_tC_%2815664918268%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bluetooth_Music_Streaming_-_2014_Scion_tC_(15664918268).jpg) A [car audio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_audio "Car audio") receiver playing music being streamed via Bluetooth from a smartphone

In general, multimedia content is data-intensive, so media storage and transmission costs are still significant. Media is generally [compressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression") for transport and storage. Increasing consumer demand for streaming [high-definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-definition_television "High-definition television") (HD) content has led the industry to develop technologies such as [WirelessHD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WirelessHD "WirelessHD") and [G.hn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G.hn "G.hn"), which are optimized for streaming HD content. Many developers have introduced HD streaming apps that work on smaller devices, such as tablets and smartphones, for everyday purposes.

> "Streaming creates the illusion—greatly magnified by headphone use, which is another matter—that music is a utility you can turn on and off; the water metaphor is intrinsic to how it works. It dematerializes music, denies it a crucial measure of autonomy, reality, and power. It makes music seem disposable, impermanent. Hence it intensifies the ebb and flow of pop fashion, the way [musical 'memes'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_viral_music_videos "List of viral music videos") rise up for a week or a month and are then forgotten. And it renders our experience of individual artists/groups shallower."

— [Robert Christgau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Christgau "Robert Christgau"), 2018[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-40)

A media stream can be streamed either _live_ or _on demand_. Live streams are generally provided by a method called _true streaming_. True streaming sends the information straight to the computer or device without saving it to a local file. On-demand streaming is provided by a method called _[progressive download](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_download "Progressive download")_. Progressive download saves the received information to a local file and then plays it from that location. On-demand streams are often saved to files for extended period of time, while live streams are available at one time only (e.g., during a football game).[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-41)

Streaming media is increasingly being coupled with the use of social media. For example, sites such as YouTube encourage social interaction in webcasts through features such as [live chat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_chat "Live chat"), [online surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_survey "Online survey"), user posting of comments online, and more. Furthermore, streaming media is increasingly being used for [social business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_business "Social business") and [e-learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-learning "E-learning").[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-42)

The [Horowitz Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Horowitz_Research&action=edit&redlink=1 "Horowitz Research (page does not exist)") State of Pay TV, OTT, and SVOD 2017 report said that 70 percent of those viewing content did so through a streaming service and that 40 percent of TV viewing was done this way, twice the number from five years earlier. [Millennials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennials "Millennials"), the report said, streamed 60 percent of the content.[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-43)

### Transition from DVD

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Transition from DVD")\]

One of the movie streaming industry's largest impacts was on the DVD industry, which drastically dropped in popularity and profitability with the mass popularization of online content.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-44) The rise of media streaming caused the downfall of many DVD rental companies, such as [Blockbuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockbuster_(retailer) "Blockbuster (retailer)"). In July 2015, _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ published an article about [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix "Netflix")'s DVD services. It stated that Netflix was continuing their DVD services with 5.3 million subscribers, which was a significant drop from the previous year. On the other hand, their streaming service had 65 million members.[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-45) The shift to streaming platforms also led to the decline of DVD rental services. In July 2024, [NBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC_News "NBC News") reported that [RedBox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redbox "Redbox"), a DVD rental service that had operated for 22 years, would shut down due to the rapid incline of streaming platforms. As the rental services has been rapidly declining since 2010, the business had to file for bankruptcy, with 99% of households now subscribing to streaming services. Further reflecting the shift away from physical media, [Best Buy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_Buy "Best Buy") has ceased selling DVDs.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-46)

### Napster

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Napster")\]

[Music streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_streaming "Music streaming") is one of the most popular ways in which consumers interact with streaming media. In the age of digitization, the [private consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_good "Private good") of music has transformed into a [public good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_good "Public good"), largely due to one player in the market: Napster.

[Napster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napster "Napster"), a [peer-to-peer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-to-peer "Peer-to-peer") (P2P) file-sharing network where users could upload and download [MP3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MP3 "MP3") files freely, broke all music industry conventions when it launched in early 1999 in Hull, Massachusetts. The platform was developed by Shawn and John Fanning as well as [Sean Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_Parker "Sean Parker").[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-47) In an interview from 2009, Shawn Fanning explained that Napster "was something that came to me as a result of seeing a sort of unmet need and the passion people had for being able to find all this music, particularly a lot of the obscure stuff, which wouldn't be something you go to a record store and purchase, so it felt like a problem worth solving."[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-48)

Not only did this development disrupt the music industry by making songs that previously required payment to be freely accessible to any Napster user, but it also demonstrated the power of P2P networks in turning any digital file into a public, shareable good. For the brief period of time that Napster existed, mp3 files fundamentally changed as a type of good. Songs were no longer financially excludable, barring access to a computer with internet access, and they were not rivals, meaning if one person downloaded a song, it did not diminish another user from doing the same. Napster, like most other providers of public goods, faced the [free-rider problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-rider_problem "Free-rider problem"). Every user benefits when an individual uploads an mp3 file, but there is no requirement or mechanism that forces all users to share their music. Generally, the platform encouraged sharing; users who downloaded files from others often had their own files available for upload as well. However, not everyone chose to share their files. There was no built-in incentive specifically discouraging users from sharing their own files.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-49)

This structure revolutionized the consumer's perception of ownership over [digital goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_goods "Digital goods"); it made music freely replicable. Napster quickly garnered millions of users, growing faster than any other business in history. At the peak of its existence, Napster boasted about 80 million users globally. The site gained so much traffic that many college campuses had to block access to Napster because it created network congestion from so many students sharing music files.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-expertise1-50)

The advent of Napster sparked the creation of numerous other P2P sites, including [LimeWire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LimeWire "LimeWire") (2000), [BitTorrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BitTorrent "BitTorrent") (2001), and [the Pirate Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pirate_Bay "The Pirate Bay") (2003). The reign of P2P networks was short-lived. The first to fall was Napster in 2001. Numerous lawsuits were filed against Napster by various record labels, all of which were subsidiaries of [Universal Music Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Music_Group "Universal Music Group"), [Sony Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_Music "Sony Music") Entertainment, [Warner Music Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Music_Group "Warner Music Group"), or [EMI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMI "EMI"). In addition to this, the [Recording Industry Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_Industry_Association_of_America "Recording Industry Association of America") (RIAA) also filed a lawsuit against Napster on the grounds of unauthorized distribution of copyrighted material, which ultimately led Napster to shut down in 2001.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-expertise1-50) In an interview with the _New York Times_, Gary Stiffelman, who represents [Eminem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminem "Eminem"), [Aerosmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosmith "Aerosmith"), and [TLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TLC_discography "TLC discography"), explained, "I'm not an opponent of artists' music being included in these services, I'm just an opponent of their revenue not being shared."[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-51)

#### The fight for intellectual property rights: _A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc._

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: The fight for intellectual property rights: A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc.")\]

The lawsuit _[A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26M_Records,_Inc._v._Napster,_Inc. "A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc.")_ fundamentally changed the way consumers interact with music streaming. It was argued on 2 October 2000, and was decided on 12 February 2001. The [Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Appeals "Court of Appeals") for the Ninth Circuit ruled that a P2P file-sharing service could be held liable for contributory and vicarious infringement of copyright, serving as a landmark decision for Intellectual property law.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-wustl1-52)

The first issue that the Court addressed was [fair use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_use "Fair use"), which says that otherwise infringing activities are permissible so long as they are for purposes "such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching \[...\] scholarship, or research."[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-cornell2001-53) Judge Beezer, the judge for this case, noted that Napster claimed that its services fit "three specific alleged fair uses: [sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_(music) "Sampling (music)"), where users make temporary copies of a work before purchasing; space-shifting, where users access a sound recording through the Napster system that they already own in audio CD format; and permissive distribution of recordings by both new and established artists."[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-cornell2001-53) Judge Beezer found that Napster did not fit these criteria, instead enabling their users to repeatedly copy music, which would affect the market value of the copyrighted good.

The second claim by the plaintiffs was that Napster was actively contributing to [copyright infringement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_infringement "Copyright infringement") since it had knowledge of widespread file sharing on its platform. Since Napster took no action to reduce infringement and financially benefited from repeated use, the court ruled against the P2P site. The court found that "as much as eighty-seven percent of the files available on Napster may be copyrighted and more than seventy percent may be owned or administered by plaintiffs."[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-cornell2001-53)

The [injunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injunction "Injunction") ordered against Napster ended the brief period in which music streaming was a public good – non-rival and non-excludable in nature. Other P2P networks had some success at sharing MP3s, though they all met a similar fate in court. The ruling set the precedent that copyrighted digital content cannot be freely replicated and shared unless given consent by the owner, thereby strengthening the property rights of artists and record labels alike.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-wustl1-52)

### Music streaming platforms

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Music streaming platforms")\]

Main article: [Music streaming service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_streaming_service "Music streaming service")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/Music_streaming_platform_use_vs_music_piracy_rates_in_U.S.png/250px-Music_streaming_platform_use_vs_music_piracy_rates_in_U.S.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Music_streaming_platform_use_vs_music_piracy_rates_in_U.S.png) As music streaming platforms have become more prevalent in the US, music piracy rates have fallen. Piracy rates are calculated as a function of US total population.[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-54)

Although music streaming is no longer a freely replicable public good, streaming platforms such as [Spotify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotify "Spotify"), [Deezer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deezer "Deezer"), [Apple Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Music "Apple Music"), [SoundCloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SoundCloud "SoundCloud"), [YouTube Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube_Music "YouTube Music"), and [Amazon Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Music "Amazon Music") have shifted music streaming to a [club-type good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_good "Club good"). While some platforms, most notably Spotify, give customers access to a [freemium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemium "Freemium") service that enables the use of limited features for exposure to advertisements, most companies operate under a premium subscription model.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-55) Under such circumstances, music streaming is financially excludable, requiring that customers pay a monthly fee for access to a music library, but non-rival, since one customer's use does not impair another's.

An article written by the [New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times "New York Times") in 2021 states that "streaming saved music." This is because it provided monthly revenue. Especially Spotify offers its free platform, but you can pay for their premium to get music ad-free.[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-56) This allows access for people to stream music anywhere from their devices not having to rely on CDs anymore.

There is competition between services similar but lesser to the streaming wars for video media. As of 2019[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit), Spotify has over 207 million users in 78 countries,[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-57) As of 2018[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit), Apple Music has about 60 million, and SoundCloud has 175 million.[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-58) All platforms provide varying degrees of accessibility. Apple Music and Prime Music only offer their services for paid subscribers, whereas Spotify and SoundCloud offer freemium and premium services. Napster, owned by Rhapsody since 2011, has resurfaced as a music streaming platform offering subscription-based services to over 4.5 million users as of January 2017[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit).[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-59)

In the evolving music streaming landscape, competition among platforms is shaped by various factors, including royalty rates, exclusive content, and market expansion strategies. A notable development occurred in January 2025, when Universal Music Group (UMG) and Spotify announced a new multi-year agreement. This partnership aims to enhance opportunities for artists and consumers through innovative subscription tiers and an enriched audio-visual catalog.[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-60)

The music industry's response to music streaming was initially negative. Along with music piracy, streaming services disrupted the market and contributed to the fall in US revenue from $14.6 billion in 1999 to $6.3 billion in 2009. CDs and single-track downloads were not selling because content was freely available on the Internet. By 2018, however, music streaming revenue exceeded that of traditional revenue streams (e.g. record sales, album sales, downloads).[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-:0-61) Streaming revenue is now one of the largest driving forces behind the growth in the music industry.

### COVID-19 pandemic

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: COVID-19 pandemic")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/1980-_Summer_cinema_box_office_revenue.svg/250px-1980-_Summer_cinema_box_office_revenue.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:1980-_Summer_cinema_box_office_revenue.svg) [Theater ticket sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_office#Related_terminology "Box office") declined with the rise of streaming services, and theaters did not fully recover from declines during the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic") when many were closed.[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-NYTimes_20250903-62)

By August 2020, the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic") had streaming services busier than ever. The pandemic contributed to a surge in subscriptions, in the UK alone, 12 million people joined a new streaming service that they had not previously had.[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-63) Global subscriptions skyrocketed passing 1 billion.[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-64) Within the first 3 months, back in 2020, nearly 15.7 million people signed up for Netflix.[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-65)

An impact analysis of 2020 data by the [International Confederation of Societies of Authors and Composers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Confederation_of_Societies_of_Authors_and_Composers "International Confederation of Societies of Authors and Composers") (CISAC) indicated that remuneration from digital streaming of music increased with a strong rise in digital royalty collection (up 16.6% to EUR 2.4 billion), but it would not compensate the overall loss of income of authors from concerts, public performance and broadcast.[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-66)  The [International Federation of the Phonographic Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Federation_of_the_Phonographic_Industry "International Federation of the Phonographic Industry") (IFPI) recompiled the music industry initiatives around the world related to the COVID-19. In its State of the Industry report, it recorded that the global recorded music market grew by 7.4% in 2022, the 6th consecutive year of growth. This growth was driven by streaming, mostly from paid subscription streaming revenues which increased by 18.5%, fueled by 443 million users of subscription accounts by the end of 2020.[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-67)

The COVID-19 pandemic has also driven an increase in misinformation and disinformation, particularly on streaming platforms like YouTube and [podcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast "Podcast").[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-68)

## Technologies

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Technologies")\]

### Bandwidth

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Bandwidth")\]

A broadband speed of 2 Mbit/s or more is recommended for streaming [standard-definition video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard-definition_video "Standard-definition video"),[\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-72) for example to a [Roku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roku "Roku"), [Apple TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_TV_(device) "Apple TV (device)"), [Google TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_TV_(operating_system) "Google TV (operating system)") or a Sony TV Blu-ray Disc Player. 5 Mbit/s is recommended for high-definition content and 25 Mbit/s for [ultra-high-definition content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-high-definition_television "Ultra-high-definition television").[\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-73) Streaming media storage size is calculated from the streaming bandwidth and length of the media using the following formula (for a single user and file): storage size in [megabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabyte "Megabyte") is equal to length (in seconds) × [bit rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_rate "Bit rate") (in bit/s) / (8 × 1024 × 1024). For example, one hour of digital video encoded at 300 kbit/s (this was a typical broadband video in 2005 and it was usually encoded in 320 × 240 resolution) will be: (3,600 s × 300,000 bit/s) / (8 × 1024 × 1024) requires around 128 [MB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabyte "Megabyte") of storage.

If the file is stored on a server for on-demand streaming and this stream is viewed by 1,000 people at the same time using a [Unicast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicast "Unicast") protocol, the requirement is 300 kbit/s × 1,000 = 300,000 kbit/s = 300 Mbit/s of bandwidth. This is equivalent to around 135 [GB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigabyte "Gigabyte") per hour. Using a [multicast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multicast "Multicast") protocol the server sends out only a single stream that is common to all users. Therefore, such a stream would only use 300 kbit/s of server bandwidth.

In 2018 video was more than 60% of data traffic worldwide and accounted for 80% of growth in data usage.[\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-74)[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-75)

### Protocols

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Protocols")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/Unicast_streaming.svg/250px-Unicast_streaming.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Unicast_streaming.svg) Unicast connections require multiple connections from the same streaming server even when it streams the same content.

Video and audio streams are compressed to make the file size smaller. [Audio coding formats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_coding_format "Audio coding format") include [MP3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MP3 "MP3"), [Vorbis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorbis "Vorbis"), [AAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Audio_Coding "Advanced Audio Coding") and [Opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_(audio_format) "Opus (audio format)"). [Video coding formats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_coding_format "Video coding format") include [H.264](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.264 "H.264"), [HEVC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HEVC "HEVC"), [VP8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VP8 "VP8"), [VP9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VP9 "VP9") and [AV1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AV1 "AV1"). Encoded audio and video streams are assembled in a container [bitstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstream "Bitstream") such as [MP4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPEG-4 "MPEG-4"), [FLV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FLV "FLV"), [WebM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WebM "WebM"), [ASF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Systems_Format "Advanced Systems Format") or [ISMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Streaming_Media_Alliance "Internet Streaming Media Alliance"). The bitstream is delivered from a streaming server to a streaming client (e.g., the computer user with their Internet-connected laptop) using a transport protocol, such as Adobe's [RTMP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-Time_Messaging_Protocol "Real-Time Messaging Protocol") or [RTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_Transport_Protocol "Real-time Transport Protocol").

In the 2010s, technologies such as Apple's [HLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP_Live_Streaming "HTTP Live Streaming"), Microsoft's Smooth Streaming, Adobe's HDS and non-proprietary formats such as [MPEG-DASH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPEG-DASH "MPEG-DASH") emerged to enable [adaptive bitrate streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_bitrate_streaming "Adaptive bitrate streaming") over [HTTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP "HTTP") as an alternative to using proprietary transport protocols. Often, a streaming transport protocol is used to send video from an event venue to a [cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing "Cloud computing") transcoding service and [content delivery network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_delivery_network "Content delivery network"), which then uses HTTP-based transport protocols to distribute the video to individual homes and users.[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-76) The streaming client (the end user) may interact with the streaming server using a control protocol, such as [MMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Media_Server "Microsoft Media Server") or [RTSP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTSP "RTSP").

The quality of the interaction between servers and users is based on the workload of the streaming service; as more users attempt to access a service the quality may be affected by resource constraints in the service.[\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-77) Deploying clusters of streaming servers is one such method where there are regional servers spread across the network, managed by a singular, central server containing copies of all the media files as well as the [IP addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address "IP address") of the regional servers. This central server then uses [load balancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_balancing_(computing) "Load balancing (computing)") and [scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduling_(computing) "Scheduling (computing)") algorithms to redirect users to nearby regional servers capable of accommodating them. This approach also allows the central server to provide streaming data to both users as well as regional servers using [FFmpeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FFmpeg "FFmpeg") libraries if required, thus demanding the central server to have powerful data processing and immense storage capabilities. In return, workloads on the streaming backbone network are balanced and alleviated, allowing for optimal streaming quality.[\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-78)\[ _[needs update](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_\]

Designing a network protocol to support streaming media raises many problems. [Datagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datagram "Datagram") protocols, such as the [User Datagram Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_Datagram_Protocol "User Datagram Protocol") (UDP), send the media stream as a series of small packets. This is simple and efficient; however, there is no mechanism within the protocol to guarantee delivery. It is up to the receiving application to detect loss or corruption and recover data using [error correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction "Error correction") techniques. If data is lost, the stream may suffer a [dropout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropout_(communications) "Dropout (communications)"). The [Real-Time Streaming Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-Time_Streaming_Protocol "Real-Time Streaming Protocol") (RTSP), [Real-time Transport Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_Transport_Protocol "Real-time Transport Protocol") (RTP) and the [Real-time Transport Control Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_Transport_Control_Protocol "Real-time Transport Control Protocol") (RTCP) were specifically designed to stream media over networks. RTSP runs over a variety of transport protocols,[\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-79) while the latter two are built on top of UDP.

HTTP adaptive bitrate streaming is based on HTTP progressive download, but contrary to the previous approach, here the files are very small, so that they can be compared to the streaming of packets, much like the case of using RTSP and RTP.[\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-80) Reliable protocols, such as the [Transmission Control Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_Control_Protocol "Transmission Control Protocol") (TCP), guarantee correct delivery of each bit in the media stream. It means, however, that when there is data loss on the network, the media stream stalls while the protocol handlers detect the loss and retransmit the missing data. Clients can minimize this effect by buffering data for display. While delay due to buffering is acceptable in video-on-demand scenarios, users of interactive applications such as video conferencing will experience a loss of fidelity if the delay caused by buffering exceeds 200 ms.[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-81)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/Multicast_stream.svg/250px-Multicast_stream.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Multicast_stream.svg) Multicasting broadcasts the same copy of the multimedia over the entire network to a group of clients.

[Unicast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicast "Unicast") protocols send a separate copy of the media stream from the server to each recipient. Unicast is the norm for most Internet connections but does not scale well when many users want to view the same television program concurrently. [Multicast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multicast "Multicast") protocols were developed to reduce server and network loads resulting from duplicate data streams that occur when many recipients receive unicast content streams independently. These protocols send a single stream from the source to a group of recipients. Depending on the network infrastructure and type, multicast transmission may or may not be feasible. One potential disadvantage of multicasting is the loss of [video on demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand "Video on demand") functionality. Continuous streaming of radio or television material usually precludes the recipient's ability to control playback. However, this problem can be mitigated by elements such as caching servers, digital [set-top boxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set-top_box "Set-top box"), and buffered [media players](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_player_(application_software) "Media player (application software)").

[IP multicast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_multicast "IP multicast") provides a means to send a single media stream to a group of recipients on a [computer network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"). A connection management protocol, usually [Internet Group Management Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Group_Management_Protocol "Internet Group Management Protocol"), is used to manage the delivery of multicast streams to the groups of recipients on a LAN. One of the challenges in deploying IP multicast is that routers and firewalls between LANs must allow the passage of packets destined to multicast groups. If the organization that is serving the content has control over the network between server and recipients (i.e., educational, government, and corporate [intranets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intranet "Intranet")), then routing protocols such as [Protocol Independent Multicast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocol_Independent_Multicast "Protocol Independent Multicast") can be used to deliver stream content to multiple [local area network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_area_network "Local area network") segments.

[Peer-to-peer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-to-peer "Peer-to-peer") (P2P) protocols arrange for prerecorded streams to be sent between computers. This prevents the server and its network connections from becoming a bottleneck. However, it raises technical, performance, security, quality, and business issues.

[Content delivery networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_delivery_network "Content delivery network") (CDNs) use intermediate servers to distribute the load. Internet-compatible unicast delivery is used between CDN nodes and streaming destinations.

### Recording

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Recording")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/Keanin_Loomis%2C_livestream_%2852045467717%29.jpg/250px-Keanin_Loomis%2C_livestream_%2852045467717%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Keanin_Loomis,_livestream_(52045467717).jpg) A candidate being livestreamed with a smartphone, in the [2022 Hamilton, Ontario, municipal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Hamilton,_Ontario,_municipal_election "2022 Hamilton, Ontario, municipal election")

Media that is livestreamed can be recorded through certain media players, such as [VLC player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLC_player "VLC player"), or through the use of a [screen recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_recorder "Screen recorder"). Live-streaming platforms such as [Twitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitch_(service) "Twitch (service)") may also incorporate a [video on demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand "Video on demand") system that allows automatic recording of live broadcasts so that they can be watched later.[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-82) YouTube also has recordings of live broadcasts, including television shows aired on major networks. These streams have the potential to be recorded by anyone who has access to them, whether legally or otherwise.[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-83)

Recordings can happen through any device that allows people to watch movies they do not have access to or be at a music festival they could not get tickets to. These live streaming platforms have revolutionized entertainment, creating new ways for people to interact with content. Many celebrities started live streaming during COVID-19 through platforms like [Instagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instagram "Instagram"), [YouTube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube "YouTube"), and [TikTok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TikTok "TikTok") offering an alternate form of entertainment when concerts were postponed. Live streaming and recording allow for fans to communicate with these artists through chats and likes.

### View recommendation

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: View recommendation")\]

Most streaming services feature a [recommender system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recommender_system "Recommender system") for viewing based on each user's view history in conjunction with all viewers' aggregated view histories. Rather than focusing on subjective categorization of content by content curators, there is an assumption that, with the immensity of data collected on viewing habits, the choices of those who are first to view content can be algorithmically extrapolated to the totality of the user base, with increasing probabilistic accuracy as to the likelihood of their choosing and enjoying the recommended content as more data is collected.[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-84)

## Applications and marketing

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Applications and marketing")\]

Useful and typical applications of streaming are, for example, long [video lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_lesson "Video lesson") performed online.[\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-85) An advantage of this presentation is that these lectures can be very long, although they can always be interrupted or repeated at arbitrary places. Streaming enables new content marketing concepts. For example, the [Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Philharmonic_Orchestra "Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra") sells Internet live streams of whole concerts instead of several CDs or similar fixed media in their [Digital Concert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Concert_Hall "Digital Concert Hall")[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-86) using YouTube for [trailers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailer_(promotion) "Trailer (promotion)"). These online concerts are also spread over a lot of different places, including cinemas at various places on the globe. A similar concept is used by the [Metropolitan Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Opera "Metropolitan Opera") in New York. There is also [a livestream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Definition_Earth_Viewing_cameras "High Definition Earth Viewing cameras") from the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station").[\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-87)[\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-88) In video entertainment, video streaming platforms like [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix "Netflix"), [Hulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulu "Hulu"), and [Disney+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney%2B "Disney+") are mainstream elements of the media industry.[\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-89)

Marketers have found many opportunities offered by streaming media and the platforms that offer them, especially in light of the significant increase in the use of streaming media during [COVID lockdowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID_lockdowns "COVID lockdowns") from 2020 onwards. While revenue and placement of [traditional advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising "Advertising") continued to decrease, [digital marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_marketing "Digital marketing") increased by 15% in 2021,[\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-90) with [digital media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media "Digital media") and [search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine "Search engine") representing 65% of the expenditures.

A case study commissioned by the WIPO[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-91) indicates that streaming services attract advertising budgets with the opportunities provided by interactivity and the use of data from users, resulting in personalization on a mass scale with [content marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_marketing "Content marketing").[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-92) Targeted marketing is expanding with the use of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), in particular programmatic advertisement, a tool that helps advertisers decide their campaign parameters and whether they are interested in buying advertising space online or not. One example of advertising space acquisition is Real-Time Bidding (RTB).[\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-93)

## Challenges

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Challenges")\]

### Copyright issues

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Copyright issues")\]

See also: [Copyright aspects of downloading and streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_aspects_of_downloading_and_streaming "Copyright aspects of downloading and streaming")

For [over-the-top media service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-top_media_service "Over-the-top media service") (OTT) platforms, the original content captures additional subscribers.[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-94) This presents copyright issues and the potential for international exploitation through streaming,[\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-95) widespread use of standards, and metadata in digital files.[\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-96) The WIPO has indicated several basic copyright issues arising for those pursuing work in the film[\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-97) and music industries[\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-98) in the era of streaming.

Streaming copyrighted content can involve making infringing copies of the works in question. The recording and distribution of streamed content is also an issue for many companies that rely on revenue based on views or attendance.[\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-99)

### Greenhouse gas emissions

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Streaming_media&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Greenhouse gas emissions")\]

Main article: [Greenhouse gas emissions § Digital services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions#Digital_services "Greenhouse gas emissions")

The net [greenhouse gas emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions "Greenhouse gas emissions") from streaming music were estimated at between 0.2 and 0.35 million metric tons [CO2eq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CO2eq "CO2eq") (between 200,000 and 340,000 long tons; 220,000 and 390,000 short tons) per year in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions_by_the_United_States "Greenhouse gas emissions by the United States"), by a 2019 study.[\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-100) This was an increase from emissions in the pre-digital music period, which were estimated at "0.14 million metric tons (140,000 long tons; 150,000 short tons) in 1977, 0.136 million (134,000 long tons; 150,000 short tons) in 1988, and 0.157 million (155,000 long tons; 173,000 short tons) in 2000."[\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-101) However, this is far less than other everyday activities such as eating. For example [greenhouse gas emissions in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions_in_the_United_States "Greenhouse gas emissions in the United States") from beef cattle ( [burping of ruminants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burping "Burping") only - not including their [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure "Manure")) were 129 million metric tons (127 million long tons; 142 million short tons) in 2019.[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-102)

A 2021 study claimed that, based on the amount of data transmitted, one hour of streaming or videoconferencing "emits 150–1,000 grams (5–35 oz) of carbon dioxide ... requires 2–12 liters (0.4–2.6 imp gal; 0.5–3.2 U.S. gal) of water and demands a land area adding up to about the size of an [iPad Mini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPad_Mini "IPad Mini")." The study suggests that turning the camera off during video calls can reduce the greenhouse gas and water use footprints by 96%, and that an 86% reduction is possible by using standard definition rather than high definition when streaming content with apps such as [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix "Netflix") or [Hulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulu "Hulu").[\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-103)[\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-104) However, another study estimated a relatively low amount of 36 grams per hour (1.3 ounces per hour), and concluded that watching a Netflix video for half an hour emitted only the same amount as driving a gasoline-fuelled car for about 100 meters (330 ft), so not a significant amount.[\[105\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-105)

One way to decrease greenhouse gas emissions associated with streaming music is to make [data centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_center "Data center") [carbon neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_neutral "Carbon neutral") by converting to electricity produced from [renewable sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy "Renewable energy"). On an individual level, the purchase of a physical CD may be more environmentally friendly if it is to be played more than 27 times.[\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-EnvironmentalImpact-106)\[ _[dubious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Accuracy_dispute#Disputed_statement "Wikipedia:Accuracy dispute") – [discuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Streaming_media#Dubious "Talk:Streaming media")_\] Another option for reducing energy use is downloading the music for offline listening to reduce the need for streaming over distance.[\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-EnvironmentalImpact-106) The Spotify service has a built-in local cache to reduce the necessity of repeating song streams.[\[107\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media#cite_note-107)
